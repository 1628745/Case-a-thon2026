Shelf-Life Guard

Bad data quietly makes delivery promises wrong. We built a safeguard that fixes most of it.

When a courier picks up a package, the system tells the customer when it will arrive. That estimate comes from an AI model that relearns every week from recent deliveries. If the delivery records feeding it go bad, the promises go bad too, and nothing on screen warns anyone.

Backed by trained model updates across experiments on real deliveries from Chongqing, all scored against the real delivery times.

Each update retrains the models from scratch in about seconds, so they're easy to keep current as new data comes in.

Part 1 of 3

What goes wrong with the data

Old data wasn't the real problem. Unreliable sources were.

Nobody hacked anything. We recreated three ordinary ways delivery data goes bad and measured what each one did to the promises.

Outdated data

Old history piles up

The system keeps learning from every past week, even months-old ones, as if they mattered as much as last week. It's what happens when nobody cleans up a data pipeline.

Outdated data

Stats stop updating

The courier and neighborhood averages the model reads stop refreshing at the end of June, and the model keeps using them. It's what happens when a nightly job fails and nobody notices.

Sloppy scans

Sloppy scanning

couriers stop scanning at the door. They mark a whole stack delivered at the end of the shift, and their device uploads some records twice. To the model, their deliveries look hours slower than they really were.

Old data wasn't the problem

Learning from more history never made predictions worse. Where the data comes from matters far more than how old it is.

How it works: exactly what we changed

Every change was applied to an in-memory copy of the LaDe Chongqing delivery data and written to a modification log. The untouched data is the reference. Scoring always uses the real delivery times.

  • Outdated data (“stale”): training uses all past weeks at equal weight instead of the last , and the courier and area history features are frozen with data through : predictions used stale stats.
  • Sloppy scans (“low quality”): of couriers (). Bulk scans move the delivery time to the courier's last scan that day, for of their packages in week , rising to . From week , of their records are uploaded twice: wrong times and duplicates in all.
  • Both at once (“combined”): all of the above together. We also tested this.

Part 2 of 3

What we built: the Guard

Three checks, placed where bad data gets in.

For each package, the prediction system gives the most likely delivery time and the promise: a time we expect to beat 4 times out of 5. It relearns from the last weeks of deliveries every week. The Guard wraps around that loop.

1 Check at the door

Every new record is screened for telltale signs: many packages marked delivered in the same minute, the same package uploaded twice, impossible times or distances.

Suspicious records aren't deleted. They count for only as much, so nothing is lost if the check is wrong.

2 Weigh by trust

Recent records count more than old ones. Couriers whose records keep disagreeing with others working the same area at the same hour count less.

Old stats are kept, because they were still useful. A person gets an alert instead.

3 Final check before promising

The promise is tuned to be on time about of the time.

If a package is unusual, like a brand-new area or a prediction the model is very unsure about, a dispatcher decides instead of the system.

How it works: the technical version

The prediction system is two LightGBM gradient-boosted regressors: a median model and an 80th-percentile (P80) model, predicting delivery duration in minutes. Both are refit from scratch every week on the previous weeks.

  • L1 ingest checks: + packages from one courier scanned in the same minute, a repeated package id, a non-positive duration, a GPS fix older than min, or a courier more than km from the stop. Flagged rows get training weight × and are excluded from history features. The checks never read the injection labels.
  • L2 trust-weighted training: weight = recency × reputation × flag. Recency halves every weeks. Reputation comes from the courier's flag rate and robust disagreement with peers in the same area × hour, shrunk toward neutral with a -record prior and applied at λ = . Per-courier weight is capped at × the median.
  • L3 serve-time check: conformal P80 calibrated per area type on the most recent week. Defer when the area is unseen in the window, GPS is stale, or the P80 − median interval is more than × the area type's median width.
