Bad data quietly makes delivery promises wrong.

When a courier picks up a package, the system tells the customer when it will arrive. That estimate comes from an AI model that relearns every week from recent deliveries. If the delivery records feeding it go bad, the promises go bad too, and nothing on screen warns anyone.

We tested this on real deliveries from Chongqing, broke the data in realistic ways, and built a safeguard, the Guard, that catches most of the damage.

Old data wasn't the problem

We expected data to have a shelf life: too little history is noisy, too much is out of date. That's not what happened. More history never made predictions worse, and stats that stopped updating cost nothing ( min).

What does go stale is the model. One that's never retrained gets minutes worse per package. Where the data comes from mattered far more than how old it was.

How it works: exactly what we changed

Every change was applied to an in-memory copy of the LaDe Chongqing delivery data and written to a modification log. The untouched data is the reference. Scoring always uses the real delivery times.

Part 2 of 3

What we built: the Guard

Three checks, placed where bad data gets in.

For each package, the prediction system gives two numbers: the most likely delivery time and the promise, a time we expect to beat 4 times out of 5. It relearns from the last weeks of deliveries every week. The Guard wraps around that loop.

1 Check at the door

Every new record is screened for telltale signs: many packages marked delivered in the same minute, the same package uploaded twice, impossible times or distances.

Suspicious records aren't deleted. They count for only as much, so nothing is lost if the check is wrong.

2 Weigh by trust

Recent records count more than old ones. Couriers whose records keep disagreeing with others working the same area at the same hour count less.

Old stats are kept, because they were still useful. A person gets an alert instead.

3 Final check before promising

The promise is tuned to be on time about of the time.

If a package is unusual, like a brand-new area or a prediction the model is very unsure about, a dispatcher decides instead of the system.

How it works: the technical version

The prediction system is two LightGBM gradient-boosted regressors: a median model and an 80th-percentile (P80) model. Both are refit from scratch every week on the previous weeks.

  • L1 ingest checks: + packages from one courier scanned in the same minute, a repeated package id, a non-positive duration, a GPS fix older than min, or a courier more than km from the stop. Flagged rows get training weight × and are excluded from history features. The checks never read the injection labels.
  • L2 trust-weighted training: weight = recency × reputation × flag. Recency halves every weeks. Reputation comes from the courier's flag rate and robust disagreement with peers in the same area × hour, shrunk toward neutral with a -record prior and applied at λ = . Per-courier weight is capped at × the median.
  • L3 serve-time check: conformal P80 calibrated per area type on the most recent week. Defer when the area is unseen in the window, GPS is stale, or the P80 − median interval is more than × the area type's median width.

Where it falls short

Only fixed

The surprise problem got through

Couriers who mark packages delivered early leave no obvious trace. The check at the door caught only of their records, and the Guard removed almost none of the extra minutes they caused.

Next fix (not tested yet): stop using a low-trust courier's own stats, and watch for delivery times that suddenly get shorter.

Longer promises

On clean data, promises got a little longer

To be on time about of the time, the Guard's promise runs longer on clean data ( → min). That misses one of our own goals, and we're reporting it as a miss.

Trade-offs

What it costs to run

    Our goals, set before we saw the results

      How it works: full scorecard with exact values

      What we recommend next: a small, safe pilot

      1. Run it quietly for 4 weeks in one district. The Guard makes its promises alongside the current process. Dispatchers see them but don't act on them.
      2. Check in at weeks 2 and 4. The model owner checks that promises stay on time 77–83% of the time, no more than 10% of packages need a person, and no area gets more than 5% worse. We also measure how long reviews really take.
      3. Go or no-go at week 4. The operations manager decides.
      4. Stop early if on-time promises drift outside 75–85% two weeks in a row, more than 15% of packages need a person in a week, any area gets 10% worse, or the data team falls more than a week behind.

      Don't use it when

      • all the data comes from a single source: the Guard needs others to compare against;
      • the problem leaves no trace in the scans, like the early scanners;
      • nobody is staffed to handle the flagged packages;
      • a new city has no history yet: it needs a few weeks first.
