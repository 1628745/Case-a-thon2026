When does more delivery data make a promise worse?

A dispatcher in Chongqing accepts a package and has to tell the customer when it will arrive. Two LightGBM models, retrained every week on recent deliveries, set that promise. Whatever enters the weekly feed enters the promise.

We degraded that feed the way real operations do, offline, on six months of real LaDe deliveries. Then we measured what broke and built a Guard that undoes most of it. We also show where it doesn't.

Old data turned out not to be the problem

We expected a shelf-life curve: too little history is noisy, too much is out of date. It never appeared. Longer training windows never hurt, and frozen features cost nothing ( min MAE vs the reference).

What does decay is the model. One trained once and never retrained loses min. In this feed, the source's reputation matters far more than its age.

Development weeks 4–21, seed 0. MAE is mean absolute error of the expected delivery duration, in minutes; lower is better.

What we built

Two gradient-boosted models predict each package: the median duration (what we expect) and the 80th percentile (the promise). Both are refit from scratch every week on the previous weeks. The Guard wraps that loop in three layers, one per place the poison gets in.

L1 Ingest checks

Label-free tests on every incoming record: or more packages scanned by one courier in the same minute, a repeated package id, a non-positive duration, a GPS fix older than min, or a courier more than km from the stop.

Flagged records are quarantined, not deleted: weight × in training, left out of the history features.

L2 Trust-weighted training

Each record's weight = recency × source reputation × flag. Recency halves every weeks. A courier's reputation comes from their flag rate and how far their durations sit from other couriers in the same area and hour, shrunk toward neutral for couriers with few records.

Stale history is kept, because it stayed informative. A feature-store alert goes to a person instead.

L3 Serve-time promise and deferral

The promise is calibrated (conformal P80) on the most recent week, per area type, so it lands near on-time.

A package goes to a dispatcher instead of getting an automatic promise when its area is new, its GPS is stale, or its interval is more than × its area type's usual width.

The one classifier: L1 ingest flags

The delivery-time models are regressors, scored by error in minutes and promise-miss rate. We don't turn them into a classification task. The only binary decision in the system is L1's: is this incoming training record flagged or clean?

Positive class
A record the injector changed: a bulk-scanned delivery time or a duplicate re-upload (an early scan, for the held-out defect). The label is used only to score the flags; the Guard never reads it.
Prediction
Flagged by any L1 check.
What it drives
Flagged records keep weight × in training, are left out of history features, and raise their courier's flag rate in L2's reputation. No package is rejected on a flag.

Where it fails

recovered

The early scanner got past every layer

L1 caught of its records: no burst, no duplicate, nothing to see. Reputation reweights training records, but the early scanners' shortened history features reach the model directly. The MAE gap was min, and the Guard closed almost none of it.

Untested next fix: let low reputation also gate a courier's history features, and add a check for labels that shift short.

Wider promise

On clean data, the calibrated promise costs width

The unguarded model already misses about of promises. Calibrating to on-time needs a longer promise: reference → min (), stale ; on development weeks . That fails our own Promise-quality line, and we kept the strict reading.

What the Guard costs

    Next step: a bounded pilot

    1. Shadow mode, one district, 4 weeks. The Guard computes promises and deferrals next to the current process; dispatchers see them but don't act on them.
    2. Checkpoints at weeks 2 and 4, run by the model owner: miss rate 17–23%, deferrals at or under 10%, no area type more than 5% worse, steward queue cleared weekly, and real review time measured.
    3. Go / no-go at week 4, decided by the operations manager.
    4. Stop if miss rate leaves 15–25% two weeks running, deferrals pass 15% in a week, an area type gets 10% worse, or the steward backlog is older than a week.

    Don't use this system when

    • labels come from a single source: reputation needs peers to compare against;
    • the defect leaves no trace in scans, GPS or timing, like the early scanner;
    • nobody is staffed to handle deferrals and review quarantined sources;
    • a new city has no history yet: recency, reputation and calibration all need a few weeks.

    Not testable on this data: GPS-age checks (GPS fix age is 0 in every LaDe row) and label latency (we assume last week's labels have all arrived).
